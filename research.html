Dissertation

When Social Norms Fail: Information Frictions, Trust, and Environmental Cooperation

My dissertation investigates how information frictions and policy interventions jointly shape the effectiveness of social norms in energy and natural resource cooperation—and at what cost to the trust foundation on which those norms depend. Chapter 1 establishes a benchmark: under full information, which dimensions of norm credibility sustain pro-environmental behavior? Chapter 2 introduces an information friction and shows, in an incentivized laboratory experiment, how strategic misreporting erodes trust and breaks down norm-based cooperation. Chapter 3 studies the policy response—mandatory disclosure that repairs short-run behavior but may further erode the trust that makes norms self-sustaining.

Work in Progress

Chapter 1: Norm Credibility and Environmental Cooperation under Full Information

A reduced-form model plus a 2×2×2 survey experiment asking which dimensions of norm credibility—source credibility, peer representativeness, and information precision—sustain household energy conservation when information is transparent.

Chapter 2: Information Frictions, Misreporting, and the Erosion of Trust

An incentivized laboratory experiment (2×2: audit vs. no audit × honesty norm vs. contribution norm) in which senders report information to receivers over repeated rounds. Tracks misreporting behavior and the trajectory of receivers' trust to study how norm- sustained cooperation breaks down under information frictions.

Chapter 3: The Cost of Policy Intervention

A survey experiment varying trust levels and the type of mandatory disclosure intervention, studying how policy interventions that repair short-run behavior may crowd out the intrinsic motivation that sustains cooperation in high-trust environments.
